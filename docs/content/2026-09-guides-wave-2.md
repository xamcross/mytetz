### New sources, opened and confirmed in this session

1. **Sason, H., Michalsky, T., & Mevarech, Z. R. (2020).** "Promoting Middle School Students' Science Text Comprehension via Two Self-Generated 'Linking' Questioning Methods." *Frontiers in Psychology*, 11, Article 595745.
   Opened directly at `https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7649286/`.
   Sample: 193 ninth-grade students (89 boys, 104 girls; mean age 15). Three groups: Within-Text self-questioning (n=61), Extra-Text self-questioning (n=57), control with no training (n=75).
   Result: at the immediate post-test, the Within-Text group scored higher on science literacy than the other two groups, F(2,190) = 24.39, p < 0.001, η² = 0.20. The advantage over the control group held four months later on the same measure (η² = 0.13).
   Used on: `/guides/how-to-explain-a-text-to-yourself-while-you-read`.

2. **Greving, C. E., & Richter, T. (2019).** "Distributed Learning in the Classroom: Effects of Rereading Schedules Depend on Time of Test." *Frontiers in Psychology*, article, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02517.
   Opened directly at `https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6333692/`.
   Sample: 191 seventh-grade students across eight classes in three German schools (147 in the final analysis). Each student reread a text on the bacterial cell either immediately or one week later. Recall and comprehension were tested 4–5 minutes, or one week, after the second reading.
   Result: at the short test, immediate (massed) rereading beat the one-week (distributed) reread. At the one-week test, the two conditions were equal, because the massed group forgot roughly half of what the short test had shown, while the distributed group showed almost no drop between the short and the long test.
   Used on: `/guides/how-many-times-should-you-reread-something`.

3. **Miller, G. A. (1956).** "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two." *Psychological Review*, 63(2), 81–97. DOI 10.1037/h0043158.
   Opened directly at `https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/`.
   No human sample size applies; the paper is a synthesis of Miller's own experiments and prior data on memory span.
   Result: immediate memory holds about seven items, plus or minus two, and the limit applies to "chunks" of information and not to raw bits. A reader who groups a hard passage into fewer, larger chunks keeps more of it in mind at once.
   Used on: `/guides/how-to-understand-a-difficult-text`.

4. **Cash, T., & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2025).** "Quantifying Uncert-AI-nty: Testing the Accuracy of LLMs' Confidence Judgments." *Memory & Cognition*.
   **Corrected in round 2.** Round 1 cited Carnegie Mellon University's press page as the source, which is secondary. Round 2 opened the primary article directly, through a text-rendering proxy of the Springer page (`https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-025-01755-4`), because a raw fetch of that page returns a login wall to this session's tools. The exact quotes this session read there sit in the verification table below. The round-1 claim "10.03 of 20 hand-drawn sketches" was wrong: the paper's own Method section states "all participants saw sketches depicting the same 25 words," so the true base is 25, not 20; this session corrected the page. The round-1 claim "four tested models did not lower their confidence" was also too broad: the paper's own words name only two models, ChatGPT and Gemini, for that specific finding; the page now names only those two.
   Used on: `/guides/how-to-use-ai-to-study-without-cheating`.

5. **Digital Education Council (2026).** *AI in Higher Education Global Survey 2026*, and, separately, *AI in Higher Education Latin America Survey 2026*.
   **Corrected in round 2.** Round 1 attributed a 92% (and a wrongly-arithmetic'd "86%, up 16 points") adoption figure, a 67% daily-or-weekly figure, and a 65% shallow-learning-concern figure to the **global** survey. All three numbers, on a fresh, careful re-read, belong to the Council's own post about the **Latin America** survey, not the global one: `https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com/post/ai-adoption-is-nearly-universal-among-students-but-confidence-is-not` is that region's page. Round 2 opened the **global** survey's own resource page a second time, read it line by line, and confirmed that it states no single global adoption percentage for students at all. Round 2 also opened the Latin America survey's own announcement post directly and confirmed 92% of students and 79% of faculty "actively engaging with AI" there, from "over 30,000 responses from 29 higher education institutions" across that region. The verification table below gives the exact quote for every number this document now uses from each of the two surveys, kept clearly apart.
   Used on: `/guides/how-to-use-ai-to-study-without-cheating` and the refresh of `/guides/how-students-study-now`.

6. **Göksu, A., Wiradhany, W., & de Bruin, A. B. H. (2026).** "When to Take a Break: Comparing Effects of Systematic Short (Pomodoro) and Self-Regulated Breaks on Subjective Experience and Actual Learning." *Behavioral Sciences (Basel)*, 16(7), 1158.
   Opened directly at `https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405840/`.
   Sample: 176 university students. 90 studied with systematic short breaks (12 minutes of reading, then a 3-minute break). 86 studied with self-regulated breaks (the student chose when to pause).
   Result: no significant difference in reading-comprehension test scores between the two groups, t(174) = 1.60, p = 0.056 (systematic M = 12.93, SD = 2.75; self-regulated M = 12.23, SD = 3.05). A Bayesian analysis found anecdotal-to-moderate evidence for no difference. Students with self-regulated breaks reported higher energy, concentration and motivation, and less distraction, than students with fixed short breaks.
   Used on: `/guides/how-long-should-a-study-session-be`.

The 92% figure is real and confirmed, but it belongs to the Council's separate 2026 Latin America survey, and this document now names it as such, with that survey's own sample.

This session could not open a Digital Education Council page that states them; the full report needs a member login. These numbers do not appear on any mytetz page.
- **Round 2 correction: Miller (1956) does not support a word count for one chunk.** Round 1 told the reader to "break each long sentence into chunks of about seven words." Miller's seven is a count of chunks that immediate memory holds, not a count of words inside one chunk, so the paper does not support that instruction. The affected page now tells the reader to take one clause at a time, with no invented word count.
- **Round 2 correction: two claims attributed to Roediger & Karpicke (2006) needed a fresh check, because they were not in issue #63's own verified list.** The reused 61%/40% pair (one week later) is in that list and stays. Two further claims were not: the immediate-test values paired with the four-reads-versus-three-tests groups, and a claim that the reread group predicted a higher score for itself. This session opened the primary paper itself, through a text-rendering proxy of a Purdue University repository copy (`https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2006/2006_Roediger_Karpicke_PsychSci.pdf`), because a raw fetch of that PDF returns unreadable binary text to this session's tools. - **Round 2 correction: Greving & Richter (2019)'s exact sample size and one qualitative claim were wrong.** Round 1 stated "147 students in the final analysis." The paper's own Method section gives 145 for that specific analysis (free recall and comprehension), after two rounds of exclusion from the original 191; the affected page now gives the exact exclusion counts. Round 1 also said the spaced group "felt harder"; the paper does not use that word. It states that the distributed group reread the text more slowly, predicted lower learning success, and reported lower on-task focus; the affected page now uses that wording. Round 1 also implied the same cohort of students was tested twice; the paper's design is between-subjects (a different group of students sits in each of the four cells), so the affected page now compares groups rather than describing one group's score "falling".
