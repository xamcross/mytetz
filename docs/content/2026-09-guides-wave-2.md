## Step 2: the fact list

Rule used in this document: a claim needs a primary source that this session opened
directly (the publisher page, the DOI landing page, PubMed Central, or a freely

supports is marked **not confirmed** and does not appear on a page.

### Reused from issue #63 (already fact-checked; reused with the exact numbers)

- Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4–58. DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266. High utility: practice testing, distributed practice. Low utility: summarisation, highlighting and underlining, the keyword mnemonic, imagery use for text learning, rereading.
- Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249–255. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x. Three tests: 61% recall after one week. Four rereads: 40% after one week. At five minutes: reread 81%, tested 75%.
- Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). *Science*, 331(6018), 772–775. DOI 10.1126/science.1199327. One week later: retrieval practice 0.67, concept mapping 0.45 (d = 1.50).
- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). *Psychological Bulletin*, 132(3), 354–380. DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354. 254 studies, 14,811 participants. 47.3% correct after spaced study, 36.7% after massed study.
- Koriat, A., & Bjork, R. A. (2005). *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 31(2), 187–194. Named "illusions of competence": a page that feels familiar produces a false judgement of your own memory.
- Bjork, R. A. (1994), and Bjork, E. L., & Bjork, R. A. (2011). Named "desirable difficulty".

### New sources, opened and confirmed in this session

1. **Sason, H., Michalsky, T., & Mevarech, Z. R. (2020).** "Promoting Middle School Students' Science Text Comprehension via Two Self-Generated 'Linking' Questioning Methods." *Frontiers in Psychology*, 11, Article 595745.
   Opened directly at `https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7649286/`.
   Sample: 193 ninth-grade students (89 boys, 104 girls; mean age 15). Three groups: Within-Text self-questioning (n=61), Extra-Text self-questioning (n=57), control with no training (n=75).
   Result: at the immediate post-test, the Within-Text group scored higher on science literacy than the other two groups, F(2,190) = 24.39, p < 0.001, η² = 0.20. The advantage over the control group held four months later on the same measure (η² = 0.13).
   Used on: `/guides/how-to-explain-a-text-to-yourself-while-you-read`.

2. **Greving, C. E., & Richter, T. (2019).** "Distributed Learning in the Classroom: Effects of Rereading Schedules Depend on Time of Test." *Frontiers in Psychology*, article, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02517.
   Opened directly at `https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6333692/`.
   Sample: 191 seventh-grade students across eight classes in three German schools (147 in the final analysis). Each student reread a text on the bacterial cell either immediately or one week later. Recall and comprehension were tested 4–5 minutes, or one week, after the second reading.
   Result: at the short test, immediate (massed) rereading beat the one-week (distributed) reread. At the one-week test, the two conditions were equal, because the massed group forgot roughly half of what the short test had shown, while the distributed group showed almost no drop between the short and the long test.
   Used on: `/guides/how-many-times-should-you-reread-something`.

3. **Miller, G. A. (1956).** "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two." *Psychological Review*, 63(2), 81–97. DOI 10.1037/h0043158.
   Opened directly at `https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/`.
   No human sample size applies; the paper is a synthesis of Miller's own experiments and prior data on memory span.
   Result: immediate memory holds about seven items, plus or minus two, and the limit applies to "chunks" of information and not to raw bits. A reader who groups a hard passage into fewer, larger chunks keeps more of it in mind at once.
   Used on: `/guides/how-to-understand-a-difficult-text`.

4. **Cash, T., & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2025).** "Quantifying Uncert-AI-nty: Testing the Accuracy of LLMs' Confidence Judgments." *Memory & Cognition* (2025).
   Opened directly at `https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2025/july/ai-chatbots-remain-confident-even-when-theyre-wrong` (Carnegie Mellon University's own report of its researchers' published study; this page names the authors, the journal and the method, and quotes the exact figures below). A direct fetch of the journal page itself (`link.springer.com`) and of PubMed returned a login wall, so this session does not state a total participant count for the study; the CMU page did not give one either.
   Result, one worked example from the paper: the model Gemini predicted it would correctly name 10.03 of 20 hand-drawn sketches. It named 0.93 correct. Asked afterward how many it thought it had got right, it estimated 14.40. Across the tasks, the four tested models (ChatGPT, Gemini, Sonnet, Haiku) did not lower their confidence after a poor result, where human participants did.
   Used on: `/guides/how-to-use-ai-to-study-without-cheating`.

5. **Digital Education Council (2026).** *AI in Higher Education Global Survey 2026.*
   Opened directly at `https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com/resource-library-items/ai-in-higher-education-global-survey-2026` and at `https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com/post/ai-adoption-is-nearly-universal-among-students-but-confidence-is-not`.
   Sample, confirmed on the publisher's own resource page: 45,398 total responses, made up of 27,284 students and 18,114 faculty, across 35 countries.
   Confirmed on the publisher's own post: 92% of students report using AI, up from 86% in the 2024 Global AI Student Survey. 67% of students use AI daily or weekly. 65% of students are concerned that AI may make learning too shallow.
   Confirmed on the resource page: among students who have experienced AI in a course, only 5% say it has "transformed" how they learn; a further 28% say it "enhances" their learning. 15% of students say AI is integrated into many of their courses; 43% say a few; the rest say none.
   Used on: `/guides/how-to-use-ai-to-study-without-cheating` and the refresh of `/guides/how-students-study-now`.

6. **Göksu, A., Wiradhany, W., & de Bruin, A. B. H. (2026).** "When to Take a Break: Comparing Effects of Systematic Short (Pomodoro) and Self-Regulated Breaks on Subjective Experience and Actual Learning." *Behavioral Sciences (Basel)*, 16(7), 1158.
   Opened directly at `https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405840/`.
   Sample: 176 university students. 90 studied with systematic short breaks (12 minutes of reading, then a 3-minute break). 86 studied with self-regulated breaks (the student chose when to pause).
   Result: no significant difference in reading-comprehension test scores between the two groups, t(174) = 1.60, p = 0.056 (systematic M = 12.93, SD = 2.75; self-regulated M = 12.23, SD = 3.05). A Bayesian analysis found anecdotal-to-moderate evidence for no difference. Students with self-regulated breaks reported higher energy, concentration and motivation, and less distraction, than students with fixed short breaks.
   Used on: `/guides/how-long-should-a-study-session-be`.

### Claims checked and rejected as not confirmed

- **"88% of students use AI" (Digital Education Council, 2026).** This figure circulates on many secondary pages together with "45,398 people" and "35 countries". This session opened two pages published directly by the Digital Education Council. One gives the sample (45,398 responses, 27,284 students, 18,114 faculty, 35 countries) and confirms those two numbers. The other, the Council's own post on the same 2026 Global Survey, states adoption at **92%**, up from 86% in 2024, not 88%. A further search found "88%" attached to a different, narrower statistic in the Council's 2026 Latin America survey ("88% of students who report frequent use of AI are using ChatGPT"). The three popular numbers therefore mix two different Digital Education Council surveys. This document uses 92%, 86%, 45,398, 27,284, 18,114 and 35, because this session opened a Digital Education Council page that states each one directly. It does not use 88%.
- **Skills-erosion percentages ("19% retain less information", "22% find work harder without AI", "20% depend on it for better work").** Several secondary pages attribute these to the Digital Education Council 2026 survey. This session could not open a Digital Education Council page that states them; the full report needs a member login. These numbers do not appear on any mytetz page.
- **Kruger, J., & Dunning, D. (1999), "Unskilled and unaware of it."** A strong candidate source for `/guides/how-do-you-know-if-you-understand-something`. The journal page, the DOI landing page, and a hosted copy of the PDF were all attempted; the journal and DOI pages returned a paywall or a login wall, and the hosted PDF returned unreadable, corrupted text. No number from this paper appears on any mytetz page. The page instead reuses Koriat & Bjork (2005), Roediger & Karpicke (2006) and Dunlosky et al. (2013), all already confirmed above.
- **Chi, M. T. H., de Leeuw, N., Chiu, M. H., & LaVancher, C. (1994), "Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding."** A strong candidate source for the self-explanation page. `Cognitive Science` (Wiley), a university mirror, and a second course-site mirror were all attempted; the journal page returned a paywall, and both PDF mirrors returned unreadable, corrupted text through this session's tools. No number from this paper appears on any mytetz page. Sason, Michalsky & Mevarech (2020), opened directly, carries that page instead.
- **Plant, E. A., Ericsson, K. A., Hill, L., & Asberg, K. (2005), "Why study time does not predict grade point average."** A candidate source for a page on study session length. ScienceDirect and ResearchGate both returned a paywall to this session. ERIC gave a summary but not the sample size or the exact quantitative result. No number from this paper appears on any mytetz page. Göksu, Wiradhany & de Bruin (2026), opened directly, carries `/guides/how-long-should-a-study-session-be` instead.
