Study guides

Six guides show you how to study alone. Each guide answers one question. Each guide names the research behind its answer. The methods come from cognitive psychology, and a researcher tested each one in a controlled experiment. You can apply every method today.

The four findings that matter most

  • A test beats a second read, but only after a delay of one day or more.
  • A gap between two reviews beats two reviews in one sitting.
  • A highlighter marks a page. It does not move a fact into your memory.
  • A method that feels hard often works better than a method that feels easy.

How do we choose what goes on these pages?

We publish a claim only when a named study supports it. We give the authors, the year and the journal. We give the sample size. We give the year of the data.

We leave a number out when no reliable source exists. Two examples: nobody has measured the share of study that happens on a telephone; nobody has measured a trustworthy average for the length of a study session. So these pages state no number for either one.

Why can you trust these numbers?

Every guide links to the primary paper. You can open the paper and check the number yourself. Where a popular figure has no primary source, the guide says so and names the problem.

One example

Many sites state a 2026 completion rate for an online course. We found no named primary source for those figures. The best measurement we found covers 2017 and 2018, and it comes from a paper in Science. The guide gives that year, and it explains what the figure counts.
