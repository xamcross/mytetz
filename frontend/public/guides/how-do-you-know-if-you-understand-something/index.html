How do you know if you understand something

A feeling of familiarity is not proof of understanding; a passage you have seen twice starts to feel familiar even when your grasp of it has not moved. The real check is production: close the text, and try to explain the idea, solve a new example, or teach it to someone else, in full sentences.

Key points

  • A familiar page is not the same as an understood page. Researchers call the mix-up an "illusion of competence".
  • In a 2006 study, the group that reread a passage predicted the best memory for themselves, then recalled the least a week later.
  • Four checks force production: explain with no notes, solve a new example, teach it, or predict then check.
  • A check that fails has found your real gap, and that is a useful result, not a bad one.

Why does a page feel understood when it is not?

Illusion of competence
A false judgement about your own memory or understanding. A page, a term or a formula that you have looked at more than once starts to feel familiar, and the familiar feeling gets mistaken for a mastered fact. Koriat and Bjork named the effect in 2005.

The mix-up happens because familiarity and understanding share one early symptom: both feel easy. A passage you have read twice moves past your eyes with less resistance the second time, and that lower resistance feels like progress. The lower resistance is real. It does not tell you whether you could produce the idea with the page closed, which is the part that a test, an assignment or a real conversation will actually ask of you.

What four checks actually work?

Four checks that force production, and what each one reveals.
Check What you do What it reveals
Explain with no notes Close the text. Say the idea in your own words, in full sentences. Whether you have the idea at all, or only a familiar shape on the page.
Solve a new example Apply the idea to a case the text did not use. Whether you understood the rule, or only followed one worked case.
Teach it to someone else Explain the idea aloud to a person, or an imagined beginner, who has not seen it. The parts you gloss over when nobody stops you to ask a question.
Predict, then check Before you look, write your best guess at the answer. Then check it against the source. The exact gap between what you believe and what is true.

A short routine

  1. Close the text or your notes completely.
  2. Say the main idea in two or three sentences, with no term copied straight from the page.
  3. Apply the idea to one new example that the text did not use.
  4. If you cannot complete step 2 or step 3, you have found a real gap, not a failure.
  5. Reread only the part that covers the gap, then repeat the check.

What is the difference between familiar and understood?

Familiar means a piece of text no longer surprises you on sight. Understood means you can do something with it when the text is not there to lean on: state it, apply it, or defend it against a question. A reader can reach the first state after two reads and stay far from the second state. The four checks above test the second state directly, because each one removes the page before it asks for an answer.

What do you do when a check fails?

Treat the failed check as the useful part of the session, not as evidence that the session wasted your time. A check that fails names the exact sentence, step or term you need to return to, instead of leaving you to guess at where the gap sits inside a whole chapter. Return to that one part, read it once with the gap in mind, then repeat the same check rather than a different one, so that you know whether the specific gap closed.

What does the research show?

Rereaders overestimated their own memory

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had one group of students read a passage four times, and another group study it once and then take three recall tests. The paper reports that the group that reread the passage was more confident that it would remember the passage in one week than either group that took a test. One week later, the reread group recalled 40% of the passage, and the tested group recalled 61%. The group that felt more prepared performed worse.

Familiarity is a named, studied bias

Koriat and Bjork's 2005 paper, published in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, named this mismatch an "illusion of competence": a judgement of your own knowledge, made from how easy a page feels, rather than from whether you could reproduce its content unaided.

Testing works as a diagnostic, not only as a study method

Dunlosky and four colleagues' 2013 review rated practice testing as high utility, on the strength of evidence that a retrieval attempt improves later memory. The same act also serves as a diagnostic: an attempt that fails shows you the gap immediately, where a reread gives you no such signal.

Common questions

Why does highlighting make a page feel understood?

A highlighted sentence stands out on a later glance, and that stands out feeling is mistaken for knowledge of the sentence. The guide on what to use instead of a highlighter covers four replacements that ask for production instead of a glance.

Is confidence ever a reliable sign?

After a real check, yes: confidence that follows a passed test tracks your actual knowledge reasonably well. Before any check, confidence tracks how easy the material felt to look at, which is a weaker and less reliable signal.

What if I can explain an idea but still get a question wrong?

Add the second check: solve a new example. An explanation shows that you hold the idea in words. A new example shows whether you can apply it under a condition the text did not spell out for you.

How is this different from active recall?

Active recall, covered in how to test yourself while you read, is the practice that builds durable memory. This page is the check that tells you whether the practice, or any other study session, actually worked.
