How to use AI to study without cheating

An AI tool explains fast, and most students already use one. It also states a wrong fact with full confidence, and it does not reliably lower that confidence after a wrong answer. Use it to explain a stuck point, then close it and produce the answer yourself, in your own words, before you submit anything.

Key points

  • In a 2026 survey of 45,398 responses across 35 countries, only 15% of students said that AI is part of many of their courses, and only 5% of the students who saw it in a course called it transformational (Digital Education Council, 2026).
  • A 2025 study found that two tested models did not reliably lower their confidence after a poor result, where human participants did.
  • A finished AI answer can replace the practice that a course expects you to do.
  • Your school's own rule decides what counts as cheating, and this guide does not.

What does an AI study tool do well?

An AI study tool answers in seconds, at any hour, on almost any subject you name. It can rephrase a stuck sentence at a simpler level, work through the steps of a method, or give a second explanation when the first one from your course did not land. A 2026 global survey of 45,398 responses, from 27,284 students and 18,114 faculty, across 35 countries, found AI use spreading through courses: 15% of students said AI is integrated into many of their courses, and 43% said a few.

The same survey found a more careful picture underneath that use. Among students who had experienced AI inside an actual course, only 5% said it had "transformed" how they learn, 28% said it "enhances" their learning, 42% called it only somewhat helpful, and 24% saw no clear value. A tool that appears inside a course is not the same claim as a tool that students trust with the whole task.

What does an AI study tool get wrong?

An AI model produces its most confident-sounding sentence for a question it does not know the answer to, in the same voice it uses for a question it answers correctly. Nothing in its output marks the difference. A 2025 study tested this directly: across five experiments it asked human participants and one or more of four language models to judge their own performance on a task, before and after seeing the real result.

Confident but wrong
A model states a false fact in the same tone, and often the same fluent style, as a true one. The tone gives you no signal about which sentence to check.

One experiment showed 164 human participants, ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude Haiku the same 25 hand-drawn sketches and asked for a guess at each one. The model Gemini predicted it would name 10.03 of the 25 correctly. It named 0.93 correct, a result the paper calls significantly worse than the human participants' own accuracy. Asked afterward how many it thought it had got right, Gemini estimated 14.40, a higher guess than its own prediction before the task. The paper reports that the human participants became better calibrated after the task than before it, while ChatGPT and Gemini became less well calibrated after the task than before it.

Where is the line between using AI and cheating?

This guide draws no single line, because no single line applies everywhere. A school, a course and a single assignment each set their own rule, and the rules disagree with each other: one course allows an AI tool for a first draft and forbids it for a final answer; another forbids any AI text inside a graded submission; a third asks you to name which parts of your work an AI tool touched. Read the rule for the specific assignment in front of you, and ask your teacher when the rule is unclear. Follow that rule over any general advice on this page.

One limit applies whichever rule you follow: every explanation that mytetz gives also comes from a language model, the same class of tool that the section above describes. Treat a mytetz explanation with the same care: check a fact that matters for a grade against your course material or your teacher, and follow your school's rule before you submit any work that an AI tool, including this one, helped you write.

How do you use AI without losing the practice you need?

A course sets you a hard problem so that you build the skill of solving a hard problem. An AI tool that hands you the finished answer removes the one part of the task that builds that skill. The method below keeps the tool for the part it helps with, and keeps the practice for the part that only practice builds.

Four common ways to use AI while you study, and what each one costs you.
What you do What it costs you
Ask the tool to write the full answer, then submit it. You skip the practice, and you cannot check the answer against your own work.
Ask the tool to summarise a whole chapter for you. You save time, and you also skip the retrieval effort that helps you remember the chapter later.
Attempt the problem yourself, then ask the tool to check your answer. Little cost. The check comes after your own effort, not instead of it.
Ask the tool to explain one stuck point, close it, then finish the rest yourself. Little cost. The tool removes one obstacle and leaves the rest of the work to you.

A five-step method

  1. Attempt the question yourself first, even a partial or a wrong attempt.
  2. If you are stuck at one point, ask the AI tool to explain that one point, and not the whole task.
  3. Close the tool. Write your full answer yourself, in your own words.
  4. Compare your answer against the explanation. Mark the part you missed.
  5. Check your school's rule on AI use before you submit any work that the tool touched.

What does the research show?

The 2026 global survey: a large sample, and a mixed picture

The Digital Education Council's 2026 global survey drew 45,398 responses, from 27,284 students and 18,114 faculty, across 35 countries. The council's own report states no single global percentage for how many students use AI, so this guide states none either. The report does state that only 15% of students said AI is integrated into many of their courses, 43% said a few, and 43% said none; and that, among students who had experienced AI in a course, only 5% said it "transformed" how they learn, 28% said it "enhances" their learning, 42% called it only somewhat helpful, and 24% saw no clear value.

An AI model does not reliably know when it is wrong

Cash and Oppenheimer's 2025 study, published in Memory & Cognition, ran five experiments that compared human participants against three or four language models (ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude Haiku in most of them, with Claude Sonnet added later) on tasks such as a sports-prediction task and the sketch-naming game above. The paper states that human participants became better calibrated after a task than before it, while ChatGPT and Gemini became less well calibrated after a task than before it. That gap is the direct reason to verify an AI explanation before you rely on it for a grade.

Common questions

Is it cheating to ask AI to explain something?

It depends on the rule for that assignment. Some courses allow an AI explanation during study and forbid AI text inside a graded submission. Ask your teacher when the rule is not stated, and follow the answer over any general advice on this page.

Can I trust that an AI explanation is correct?

No, not on its own. Treat it as a first draft of an answer, and check the part that matters for a grade against your course material, a primary source, or your teacher.

Does an AI explanation replace a self-test?

No. An explanation removes one obstacle, such as a confusing term. It does not replace the act of producing an answer yourself, which is the part that builds lasting memory.
