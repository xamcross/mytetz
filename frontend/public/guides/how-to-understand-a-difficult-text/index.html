How to understand a difficult text

A hard text becomes readable in four moves. First, spend ten minutes on the background of the topic before you start. Second, break each long sentence into chunks of about seven words. Third, turn each heading into a question. Fourth, cover the passage and explain it back in your own words before you read on.

Key points

  • A text feels hard mostly when you lack the background to place a new fact in.
  • Working memory holds about seven items at once, and a long sentence with no chunks overflows it (Miller, 1956).
  • A heading turned into a question gives your read a target.
  • An explanation in your own words finds the part you do not yet understand.
  • A second read with no new strategy rarely fixes the first read.

Why does a text feel hard to understand?

A sentence is not hard on its own. A sentence is hard against a particular reader's background. A doctor reads a case report with ease and a legal contract with difficulty. The words in each document sit at a similar level. The difference is the store of facts that the reader already holds, ready to link to a new one.

Prior knowledge
The facts and the connections between facts that a reader already holds about a topic. A new sentence attaches to this store, or it floats free and fades within minutes.

Two other pressures make a text feel harder than its content requires. A sentence with four clauses asks you to hold four ideas in mind at the same time, before any of them connects to something you know. A page with no visible structure gives you no place to rest your attention between ideas. Both problems have a direct fix, and the sections below give each fix in turn.

How do you prepare before you read a hard passage?

Ten minutes before the passage buys more than ten minutes inside it. Read one short overview of the topic first: a summary paragraph, a glossary entry, or one paragraph from a simpler source. The goal is not mastery. The goal is a small set of names and terms that the hard text can then attach new detail to.

Four common first moves on a hard passage, and what each one does.
First move What it does What it misses
Reread the passage straight away Feels productive, and costs little effort to start. Rated low utility in a large 2013 review; adds little without a new strategy.
Skip ahead to an easier section Keeps momentum through the rest of the text. Leaves the hard passage unread, so a later section that depends on it also fails.
Look up every unfamiliar word Removes one clear source of confusion. Breaks your attention on almost every line, and a word is rarely the real block.
Build ten minutes of background first, then chunk and question the passage Gives new facts a place to attach, and gives your attention a target. Costs ten minutes that a rereader saves at first.

A short background pass also lowers the number of new names in one sentence. A sentence that names three unfamiliar terms asks a reader to learn three definitions and one relation between them, all at once. The same sentence, read after ten minutes of background, asks the reader to learn one relation, because two of the three terms are already familiar.

How do you break a hard sentence into pieces?

Immediate memory holds a small number of chunks, not a small number of words. A chunk is one unit that you already treat as a whole: a name, a familiar phrase, a short clause you can restate in one breath. A famous 1956 paper put the limit at about seven chunks, plus or minus two. A 40-word sentence with no chunking asks for 40 slots. The same sentence, split into five clauses, asks for five.

A worked example, in five steps

  1. Read the heading, or the first sentence of the paragraph. Turn it into a question you expect the paragraph to answer.
  2. Read the paragraph once, at a normal pace. Mark each clause you do not follow, but do not stop there.
  3. Return to a long sentence. Split it at each comma and each conjunction, into pieces of about seven words each.
  4. Cover the paragraph. Say the answer to your question, in your own words, in one or two sentences.
  5. Uncover it. Check the parts you missed. Read the paragraph once more, aloud, only if you missed the main point.

Step four is the part that a straight reread skips. An explanation forces you to produce the idea instead of recognising it on the page, and production is what finds the gap.

How do you check that you understood a section?

A feeling of familiarity is not proof of understanding. A passage that you have looked at twice starts to feel familiar even when your grasp of it has not moved. The check that works is the same one that step four above uses: close the text, and produce the point in your own words. If the words come out easily and in full sentences, the section is understood well enough to move on. The guide on how you know if you understand something gives four more ways to run this check.

What does the research show?

The limit on immediate memory

George Miller's 1956 paper, "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two", set out evidence that immediate memory holds about seven items at once, and that the limit applies to chunks of information and not to raw bits. A reader who groups a hard sentence into fewer, larger chunks keeps more of the sentence in mind while working through it.

Why a straight reread adds little

A 2013 review of ten study techniques rated rereading as low utility, next to summarisation, highlighting and underlining, the keyword mnemonic, and imagery use for text learning. The review rated practice testing and distributed practice as high utility. Producing an answer, as in step four of the method above, is a form of practice testing applied to one paragraph rather than to a whole course.

What production finds that recognition misses

A 2006 experiment compared students who reread a passage four times against students who took three recall tests on it. One week later the test group recalled 61% of the passage and the reread group recalled 40%. On an immediate test, five minutes after study, the order reversed: the reread group scored 81% and the test group scored 75%. The lesson for a hard passage is the same lesson at a smaller scale: a straight reread helps for the next few minutes, and an explanation you produce yourself helps for longer.

Common questions

What do I do about one word I do not know?

Guess its meaning from the rest of the sentence first, and mark it. Look it up only after you finish the paragraph. A pause on every word breaks your grasp of the whole sentence more than a wrong guess does.

Is a second read ever a good idea?

Yes, with a change of strategy. A straight second read is rated low utility. A second read that follows a self-test, and that targets only the parts you missed, is a different act and a useful one.

Can a text stay too hard, no matter what you do?

Yes. A text that assumes a prerequisite you do not have will stay hard until you find that prerequisite. Read a simpler source on the same narrow point first, then return.
